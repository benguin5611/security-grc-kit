# A.6 People controls

The 8 controls governing the security expectations placed on the people who work for or with the organisation, before, during, and after their time with it.

---

### A.6.1 Screening

**What it requires:** People should have their background checked before they start working for the organisation, and periodically afterwards, with the depth of checking scaled to the sensitivity of the role.

**Implementation guidance:**
- Define a screening policy that ties the type and depth of checks to a role's risk tier (e.g. finance, privileged IT access, and executive roles get more checks than a standard individual contributor role).
- Run standard checks for all new starters: identity verification, right-to-work, employment history and reference checks, and education verification where relevant.
- Add criminal history and/or credit checks for roles with financial authority or highly privileged system access, subject to what's lawful in the relevant jurisdiction.
- Apply the same screening standard to contractors and third parties who will get access equivalent to an employee's, not just to direct hires.
- Set a re-screening trigger for higher-risk roles: either a fixed cadence (e.g. every 2 to 3 years) or on material role change, rather than treating screening as a one-off event at hire.
- Make completed screening a hard gate before credentials or system access are provisioned.

**Evidence an assessor would want to see:**
- A documented screening policy defining risk tiers and the checks required per tier.
- Completed screening records for a sample of recent hires, including dates and sign-off, e.g. reference-check records and a verification matrix mapping checks completed to each new hire's role risk tier.
- Evidence contractors/third parties are captured by the same or an equivalent process.
- Evidence of re-screening occurring for personnel in higher-risk roles.

### A.6.2 Terms and conditions of employment

**What it requires:** Employment and contractor agreements should spell out each party's information security responsibilities, so expectations are contractually binding from the outset rather than left to policy alone.

**Implementation guidance:**
- Build a standard information security clause into the master employment contract template covering the obligation to follow security policy, acceptable use, and the consequence of breach up to and including termination.
- Include a confidentiality clause that explicitly survives the end of employment, not just one that applies while the person is active.
- State compliance with applicable laws, codes and standards as a condition of continued employment.
- Maintain a separate contractor/consultant agreement template with equivalent security and confidentiality obligations. Don't assume employee contract terms extend to third parties by default.
- Have legal and HR jointly own periodic review and version control of both templates, and ensure new starters sign the current version before their start date.
- Link the contract's breach clause explicitly to the enforcement/disciplinary policy so there's one consistent consequence story, not two disconnected documents.

**Evidence an assessor would want to see:**
- Current signed contract templates (employee and contractor) showing the security and confidentiality clauses.
- A sample of signed contracts for recent joiners, both employee and contractor.
- Version history or review date on the contract templates.

### A.6.3 Information security awareness, education and training

**What it requires:** Everyone doing work for the organisation needs security awareness training appropriate to their role, delivered at onboarding and refreshed on an ongoing basis as policy and threats evolve.

**Implementation guidance:**
- Deliver a baseline security induction to every new starter, employee or contractor, before or immediately upon start, covering acceptable use, phishing, data handling and how to report a suspected event.
- Assign clear, distinct ownership: one function owns ensuring induction happens for every new starter, another owns ongoing refreshers and updates for the existing workforce.
- Tier the content: general awareness for all staff, deeper technical content for engineering/IT roles, and specialist training for anyone holding a formal security responsibility.
- Trigger ad hoc communications when policy changes materially, a new threat pattern emerges, or after a relevant incident, rather than relying solely on an annual cycle.
- Track completion (who, what, when) centrally so coverage can be reported on and non-completers followed up.
- Review and refresh the training content at least annually and whenever the policies it's based on change.

**Evidence an assessor would want to see:**
- Induction training content and a completion log for new starters over the last 12 months, e.g. a screenshot from a security-awareness training platform showing the policy list and an individual's acknowledgement record.
- Ongoing/refresher training completion records and the cadence they run on.
- Evidence that technical/specialist roles received role-specific training beyond the general baseline.
- Named ownership (by role) for induction versus ongoing/specialist training.

### A.6.4 Disciplinary process

**What it requires:** There needs to be a known, formal process for taking action against personnel who violate information security policy, so consequences are predictable and applied consistently.

**Implementation guidance:**
- Reference the disciplinary process in both the employment contract and the security policy so the same expectation is visible from two directions.
- Define graduated consequences, from a verbal or written warning through to termination, scaled to severity and to whether the breach was negligent or deliberate.
- State explicitly that serious breaches (deliberate data exfiltration, credential sharing, disabling security controls) can lead to summary dismissal and, where applicable, referral to law enforcement or a regulator.
- Route disciplinary cases through HR with input from security/compliance, so decisions aren't made by a line manager in isolation without context on severity.
- Apply an equivalent principle to contractors and third parties via their engagement terms (e.g. termination of contract), since formal employment disciplinary process won't apply to them directly.
- Keep an aggregated record of disciplinary actions taken for security breaches to spot recurring patterns and inform training priorities.

**Evidence an assessor would want to see:**
- A written disciplinary policy section that specifically addresses information security breaches.
- A cross-reference from the employment contract or handbook to that disciplinary policy.
- A redacted or aggregated log of security-related disciplinary actions, where any have occurred.

### A.6.5 Responsibilities after termination or change of employment

**What it requires:** Security duties that need to outlive someone's employment (confidentiality above all) must be clearly defined, communicated, and genuinely enforceable after they leave or change roles.

**Implementation guidance:**
- Write confidentiality obligations into employment and contractor agreements so they explicitly survive termination, with a stated duration or "indefinite" where appropriate.
- Define the consequence of a post-departure breach (e.g. legal action) in policy, not only in contract boilerplate, so it's visible rather than assumed.
- Require a documented re-acknowledgement of ongoing confidentiality obligations at offboarding. Don't rely solely on the acknowledgement given at hire.
- Track acknowledgement completion centrally (e.g. in the HR system) so it's auditable who has and hasn't confirmed it.
- For role changes short of full termination, review and adjust access and responsibilities so obligations tied to the old role are properly closed out.
- Fold the confidentiality acknowledgement into the standard offboarding checklist alongside asset return and access revocation.

**Evidence an assessor would want to see:**
- Contract clause language showing confidentiality obligations survive termination.
- An offboarding process/checklist that includes an acknowledgement step.
- Records (e.g. an HR system export) of completed leaver acknowledgements for a sample of recent departures.
- Evidence that role changes, not just full exits, trigger a responsibilities/access review.

### A.6.6 Confidentiality or non-disclosure agreements

**What it requires:** The organisation needs identified, documented confidentiality agreements that reflect its actual protection needs, are reviewed on a regular cycle, and are signed by everyone they should apply to.

**Implementation guidance:**
- Maintain a standard NDA/confidentiality agreement template that reflects the categories of information genuinely needing protection (customer data, IP, commercially sensitive material).
- Require signature before access is granted: folded into the employment contract for staff, and as a standalone agreement for contractors and any third party who will see confidential information (e.g. vendors during due diligence).
- Set a defined review cycle for the template (e.g. annually) with a named owner, so it doesn't go stale as the business or regulatory context shifts.
- Store signed agreements centrally (HR system or contract repository) so they can be produced on request rather than tracked down in email.
- Use mutual NDAs where information will flow both directions, and one-way agreements where it won't.
- Track expiry and renewal separately for time-bound agreements tied to a specific engagement versus indefinite ones.

**Evidence an assessor would want to see:**
- A current NDA template with a visible last-review date and named owner.
- Signed agreements for a sample of employees, contractors and third parties.
- Evidence the periodic review actually took place (review log or version history).

### A.6.7 Remote working

**What it requires:** When people work outside the organisation's premises, security measures need to be in place to protect the information they access, process or store there.

**Implementation guidance:**
- Publish a remote/flexible working policy setting minimum expectations: a secured home network, locking the screen when unattended, and clear rules on (or exclusion of) personal, unmanaged devices for sensitive work.
- Require any device used remotely to be enrolled in device management with disk encryption, up-to-date patching, endpoint protection and remote-wipe capability.
- Mandate a VPN or equivalent secure access method for reaching internal systems from outside the office rather than exposing internal services directly.
- Extend physical security expectations to remote settings: not discussing confidential matters where others can overhear, not leaving devices or printed material unattended in public places.
- Explicitly cover remote-working scenarios in the incident response and disaster recovery plans (e.g. handling a lost or stolen remote device) and in asset tracking for off-site equipment.
- Make sure remote staff know how to get help provisioning or troubleshooting security controls through the normal support channel.
- Periodically audit compliance with remote working requirements, such as checking device management enrolment coverage.

**Evidence an assessor would want to see:**
- A published remote/flexible working policy covering device, network and physical security expectations.
- Evidence of enforced device management/encryption for remote-capable devices, e.g. an MDM enrolment and compliance report showing coverage across the fleet, alongside evidence of antivirus, firewall, VPN and password-protection requirements.
- Configuration or enforcement evidence for VPN/secure remote access.
- Incident response or asset management documentation that explicitly covers remote-working scenarios.

### A.6.8 Information security event reporting

**What it requires:** Personnel need clear, accessible ways to report a suspected or observed security event quickly, feeding into a defined response process.

**Implementation guidance:**
- Offer more than one reporting channel: a ticketing/helpdesk portal, a dedicated security mailbox, and direct escalation to a line manager, so there's no single point of failure if one channel is unfamiliar or unavailable.
- Publish the channels somewhere staff will actually see them: induction material, intranet, IT support page, not buried in a policy document nobody opens.
- Set and communicate an expected reporting timeframe (e.g. "as soon as observed or suspected") so delay is understood as its own problem.
- Feed every report into a single incident management process with defined triage, ownership and severity levels, so it doesn't just sit in an inbox.
- State explicitly that reporting a suspected event, even one that turns out to be a false alarm, is encouraged and won't be penalised, to keep the reporting culture healthy.
- Track reported events by volume, channel and time-to-triage to confirm the channels are actually being used and are effective.

**Evidence an assessor would want to see:**
- Documented reporting channels referenced in onboarding or intranet material.
- An incident/event management procedure describing how reports are triaged and actioned.
- A log or ticket history of reported security events with response timestamps.
- Staff communications reinforcing early reporting with no penalty for false positives.

---

## Adapt this to your context

- Screening depth (A.6.1) is heavily jurisdiction-dependent: what's lawful and customary to check varies significantly by country and even by role type. Confirm what your jurisdiction actually permits before copying a checks list from elsewhere.
- Remote working (A.6.7) assumes a workforce that's at least partly distributed. A fully co-located team can scope this down considerably, but shouldn't delete it outright: most teams end up with at least occasional remote work, and the policy should exist before the first person needs it, not after.
- The disciplinary process (A.6.4) needs to be written by, or reviewed with, HR and legal for your specific jurisdiction: employment law varies enough that a generic disciplinary clause copied from another country's template can be unenforceable or even unlawful.
